[English sermon]

Our reflection for our adults who are here with us today — though I hope we had the opportunity to learn something about fasting in our midst, hearing the Sunday school's lesson — is about the content of today's Bible passage from the Gospel of Matthew, which leads us into the Lord's Prayer. It is an introduction that guides us into the most foundational prayer in the life of Christianity. Christ is teaching us how to pray. But his prologue to teaching us that prayer is a call to those who are penitent, prayerful, and fasting to not be hypocritical, to not be hypocritical. He's warning us so very clearly: do not be a hypocrite.

Now, what does it mean to be a hypocrite? In Armenian, I love it, it's very clear: to be a hypocrite is somebody who is fake. Very, very clear. You say one thing, you do another. What is that? But being fake. You're not being honest with yourself or others around you. So first and foremost, Christ is asking us: don't be fake during Lent. Be honest, be sincere. If your heart is in the Lenten fast, then embrace it. If you're doing it for show, then it's better that you not do it. Let's not put sin on top of sin.

However, let's talk about what the opposite of being fake is, or the opposite of hypocrisy in this biblical context. It is sincerity. We have to be sincere throughout the Lenten journey. Being sincere doesn't just mean not being fake, but it means representing the reality of yourself in its complete image. The complete image of our realness is open to God and, in a loving and faithful community, also to one another.

To be sincere — the word sincere means without wax. During that time, if a piece of pottery or something like that was broken, people would put the pieces back together and they would use wax to cover up those broken parts where the cracks were. And then they would paint it and try to pass it off like it hadn't been broken, to sell it at its original price. So being sincere means presenting something with its cracks and everything. This is the reality of a thing.

And the truth is, what we have to come to terms with during Great Lent is we have cracks. We have imperfections. We are sinners. We have weaknesses. We have to be honest about that to ourselves first and foremost, and be willing to present that to God in the full context of what it means, inviting him to come and put those pieces back together himself, which he can restore to perfection, and not through using our artificial and temporary means. And also in a loving Christian environment, to be able to be honest with each other about our own imperfections as well, so that we can help each other grow stronger and help one another grow closer to God.

This is a very high calling that we all have during this Lenten season, but it begins with sincerity. It begins with looking in the mirror and asking ourselves, what ways can I be honest about my imperfections? Not so that I can be negative or self-destructive, but so I can invite God to come into those places where I have been cracked and to fill those gaps to make me whole again for his glory, in the name of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. Amen.
