My dear faithful in Christ, it was last Sunday that we asked ourselves an important question: What happens to the Word of God when we hear it—after we hear it? Jesus said that his family is made up of those who hear the Word of God and do it. So simple, so important, so very hard.

What happens, then, when we leave this sanctuary and go into the week ahead? For those of you who were here last week, what happened after you left the sanctuary and continued into the week that came? Did you carry with you that message that God’s family consists of those who hear his word and do it, or did we forget about it?

I do not say that to pass judgment. I say it only so that all of us may build awareness in our hearts and minds of the pressure and stress we are under when we leave this sanctuary, and of how quickly we lose the power of the Word of God when we forget to give care to it.

Today the Gospel brings us into a home filled with grief. The people there were dealing with quite a bit, and in the midst of all their struggles they were challenged to hear the Word of God and do it.

Jairus had come to Jesus Christ because his daughter was dying. While Jesus was still speaking, someone came from Jairus’s house with the news that every parent would dread hearing: “Your daughter has died. Do not trouble this teacher anymore.”

Think about those words: “Do not trouble him any longer. It is too late. There is nothing more that can be done.” But Jesus says to Jairus, “Do not fear; only believe.”

He goes to the house. People are weeping and mourning. Their grief is beyond consolation. Jesus tells them that the child is not dead but sleeping. As we have often reflected when preaching on this passage, something unimaginable happens: everybody laughs at Jesus for not understanding the simple difference between sleep and death. There is an important lesson here for all of us.

Jesus takes the daughter’s hand and calls her to rise. Her spirit returns, and she wakes up. Then Jesus tells everyone to give her something to eat.

It is a remarkable detail. This is the end of this particular vignette, the story of Jesus’s miraculous resurrection of Jairus’s daughter. He says, “Give this girl something to eat.” A child has been raised from the dead. Her parents are astonished. People have been left looking like fools for not trusting in Christ. And he says, “Give her something to eat.”

Luke does not stop to explain it; he moves on. But we need to understand what Jesus has done and said. He gives this girl back to her family, and he pays attention to what she needs right now. She is alive, and she needs something to eat. The people around her can feed her. They can do something.

They could not give her life back. They did not have the power to resurrect her. Jesus has done that. He is the only one who can truly give life. But there is something for them to do right now, some way for them to show their care. There is a child before them who needs them, and what she needs is to be fed.

For the past few weeks, we have been talking about Dndes, the concept of being a steward in the Church—someone entrusted with the care of the household, in this case God’s household. There is something very important and very simple for us to consider: the care of the household continues after extraordinary moments have passed, amid the simple, day-to-day, and sometimes difficult, challenging, distracting, and demanding moments of our everyday lives.

Somebody still has to prepare a meal. Somebody has to pay attention to what we need right now, in this moment. We understand that in our families. A house cannot live on one meal, one good day, one generous gesture, one expression of love, or the times when somebody happens to remember to help. That care has to be consistent and continuous. It has to be mindful, and it has to be present.

Here is the challenge, but this is what Christ is asking Jairus’s family to do: Give this young girl what she needs right now. I have given her life, but you can give her what she needs, too. Please do your part and care for this household. The same is true for the household of God.

Before we go further, though, we have to touch upon the grief we witness in this passage. We need to hear how Christ speaks about grieving. There are people in this room today who know what I am talking about and are experiencing grief right now.

He says, “Only believe,” but that should never become an accusation against someone whose loved one has died. People pray with their whole hearts and still have to say goodbye to someone they love. Their grief is not a failure of faith.

Our hope is in Christ, who enters this particular grieving household, as well as all of ours and the household of this parish. He himself went to the cross and rose from the dead. He has not abandoned us in our suffering. We can bring him our fear, grief, and disappointment without pretending that everything is all right or that we have failed in our calling to believe.

When we stand beside someone who is grieving, we do not have to explain everything. It can sometimes feel like a responsibility or duty when we bring comfort to those who mourn, but we do not have to explain everything or give answers for things we do not understand. Sometimes we simply do not know what to say, and that is all right. We can listen. We can ask what would help. We can remember to return when the first days have passed and other people have gone back to their lives.

Recently, Yeretsgin and I traveled to Boston for her grandmother’s funeral. We all know what it is like to be part of a funeral and receive everybody’s care and attention during those difficult moments. But within a matter of days, it can seem as though we are called to return to daily life. Everyone else is ready to move on, and we are not.

The Christian is called to remember that and to be present when everybody else has moved on. That is when care becomes difficult. We need to notice. We need to respond. But then our week fills up, and the person who needed us most still needs us. We are reminded once again of Christ’s calling to feed Jairus’s daughter.

In today’s Epistle, if you were not in the sanctuary earlier when it was read from Saint Paul’s Letter to the Ephesians, I encourage you to go back and read it. Saint Paul asks us to pay attention to how we live, to live wisely, and to make the most of the time we have been given.

Most of us would say that we already have a lot to do. We have work, appointments, and people constantly depending on us. Where are we supposed to find more time? I know that I feel that way myself. Saint Paul’s question reaches deeper than that, though: What are we doing with what we have? Who receives our attention now? We can be very busy and still miss the person right in front of us who needs us most.

When Saint Paul speaks about a household, he points us to Christ’s love for the Church. Christ loved the Church, and he gave himself for the Church. Today’s passage from Saint Paul speaks of that giving, sacrifice, and devotion as a marriage. He is the groom, and we are the bride. He has fully devoted himself and every aspect of his love to our well-being. This is meant to be a model not simply of marriage, but of life in the Church.

That is the measure: love seeks the good of the person entrusted to our care. It does not use that person for comfort or control. Nothing in this passage gives anyone permission to harm another person, require someone else to be harmed, or require someone to remain unsafe. Instead, all sacrifice, devotion, and attention must be offered in love.

So what does faithful care look like here at St. John? This is what we have been discussing for the past three weeks. We have been asking how we care for this parish and how we can care for it more faithfully.

Perhaps you thought of someone you should call. This is a question I asked you last week. I do not know whether you called—maybe you did. But perhaps you could call again, because that is what continued care looks like. It means checking in. It means not forgetting.

I know this is very hard. It is a challenge for me. My attention hangs on a string at any given moment, and I do the best I can to devote it to the community and to the people I love very much. But the reason I devoted my life to the Church was that I wanted to bring myself into a way of life that would demand more of me. If it demands more of me, then it demands more of all of us, even when it is hard.

I know that making a phone call can sound like a small thing, but there is a difference between being remembered once and knowing that someone will always remember you, no matter what happens. If you have met somebody new at this parish, you could greet them again after Badarak today. If a few people always seem to be carrying the same work, you could ask them, “How can I share the burden?”

When we offer help, we need to listen to the answer. We may have a good idea of what someone needs and discover that we are wrong. Asking, “Would a meal help this week?” gives a person something specific to respond to. It also gives them room to tell you what they actually need if a meal is not it.

This is ordinary parish life. This is what it means to be part of a community and part of a church. It is easy to overlook because it does not always draw attention to itself. I know so many servants of this church who seek not to draw attention to themselves, but we have to acknowledge their service so that we all understand what we are meant to aspire to.

Think about what it means when somebody notices that you have been absent from church. That kind of attention helps make a parish a household, a place where we take responsibility for one another.

Of course, none of us can carry it all alone. Some of you are already caring for your children, your aging parents, someone who is ill, or someone going through a very difficult time. Some of you need to care for yourselves more than you have. This is not meant to be an extra burden or a way for you to prove that you love God.

Sometimes your faithful step will be to accept help. We talked about this last week, and we should continue talking about it. It means taking the ride when somebody offers it. It means telling somebody honestly what you are going through and letting them participate in the challenges of your life. We belong to one another in those moments, too.

The girl in today’s Gospel received care. Her family received care. They were not asked to earn it. This is important for us to remember: the household belongs to God. This church and this parish belong to God, and we are all welcome to be part of it through baptism and chrismation—not because we have earned it, not because we are liked or favored, but because he welcomed and received us.

God’s gift always comes first. Our care for this parish grows out of that belonging. We are not buying a place in God’s family, and we are not trying to make ourselves worthy of his love, because we cannot. We respond to the love we have already received. We worship, participate, offer our time and service, and give according to our means. These are ways of caring for a life that Christ has already blessed.

Next Sunday we will talk more about how we can renew this commitment to be a Dndes in the Church on the eve of the Fast of Hisnag. I want you to be here then to learn more about how you can show your devotion to God and your commitment to his life in the Church.

For today, I want to leave you with something you can carry into the week ahead. Think of someone. Think of a responsibility already entrusted to your care. What would it mean for you to be more faithful there, to place your trust in God there, and to be part of God’s plan there? What is one thing you have done that you simply need to keep doing, submitting that commitment and responsibility to God?

Choose something you can actually carry. If you need help carrying it, ask for help. We are learning to care together for this household called St. John, and we need one another’s help to do it.

Let us return to the Gospel and to that home where they were mourning that child. When she was raised, she stood among her family. Jesus had done what no one else could do. Now he was telling all of them to be part of what he had done by giving her what she needed. They did not need to understand everything that had happened before they could do that. They could begin with the child before them. They could prepare her food, and they could care for her.

My dear faithful, Christ is the source of our life. Here during this Divine Liturgy, he fed us with his own body and blood through the Holy Eucharist. We receive this life together. When we leave this sanctuary, the people beside us are still our brothers and sisters. We have all shared this meal and received the care of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ.

There is much in this life that we cannot fix. There are sorrows, hurts, and wounds that we cannot take away on our own. But there is someone or something to which we can pay more attention. There is care that we can offer, and by God’s grace we can return tomorrow and offer it again.

May our Lord help us to see the person before us and to be faithful in the care he has entrusted to us. May all that we do be for the glory of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. Amen.
