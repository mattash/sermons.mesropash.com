I'm not talking about the history of it. I'm not talking about the narrative of it. I am pretty sure that most of us here, if we don't have the biblical accounts memorized, have a pretty rough shape in our minds of what took place two thousand years ago that we are here celebrating when we talk about the resurrection of Jesus Christ. I'm not talking about that. And I'm not just talking about the spiritual element either, because that is also very, very important, but that's not what I'm talking about. I'm talking about what happened cosmically. What happened to the world two thousand years ago when this event took place? What sort of thing is this?

Because if we're not careful, we will fall into the trap of reducing the feast of the resurrection of Easter to one of two things. Either it becomes an argument, a proof that Jesus was who He said that He was, or it becomes a sentiment. And sentiments are good, and they're encouraging, but it's a reassurance that suffering is not the end and that everything will somehow be okay. While both of those things are true, that really diminishes what the resurrection is really all about, because it's more than proof, it's more than comfort. Resurrection is the moment that God began to build the world again.

Let me explain what I mean. When we began our Lenten journey, for those of you that were following us on Fast and Pray, I know many of you were, or attending church during regular services, or just staying in touch with the life of the Armenian Apostolic Church, we began talking about expulsion from the Garden of Eden on the first Sunday of Lent, seven weeks ago. And we read together how God created humanity in His own image, made for communion and made for glory. And then we traced what happened next to the doubt and the disobedience and the exile. It wasn't just a legal violation, but it was a structural collapse of the way the world was intended to be. Sin didn't merely make us guilty. It fractured something in the very architecture of humanity. It introduced a decay into what was made to endure and death into what was made to live.

The prophets, they understood this. Ezekiel saw it in the valley of the dry bones, an entire people reduced to fragments, scattered and lifeless spiritually. We heard that passage just last night, for those of you who were with us when we began our Easter Eve celebrations, and God spoke through Ezekiel with a devastating question, saying, "Son of Man, can these bones live again?" And this question hangs over all of humanity. Can what has been ruined be made whole again? Can what has collapsed be rebuilt? Can the dead live?

And then came Christ. There's a moment early in the Gospel of Saint John that we sometimes read too quickly. Jesus is standing in the temple, which is the center of Israel's spiritual life and national life, the dwelling place of God among His people, and He says something that scandalized everybody who heard it. It was the structure in which God's presence dwelt among a broken humanity. When Christ says, "Destroy this temple," He's not predicting His own death. He's acknowledging the full extent of what sin has done. The entire sacred architecture of the world, body, soul, mind, and communion with God had been brought to ruin, and the temple of humanity lies in rubble. And when He says, "In three days, I'll raise it up," He's not talking about a renovation job. He's not talking about changing the windows and putting up some curtains. He's talking about rebuilding the whole thing from the foundation up.

Good Friday is the moment when the temple, the old temple, reached its final collapse. Everything that sin set in motion, violence, betrayal, abandonment, and agony, it all comes to a terrible climax on the cross. And on Holy Saturday, the rubble lies still.
