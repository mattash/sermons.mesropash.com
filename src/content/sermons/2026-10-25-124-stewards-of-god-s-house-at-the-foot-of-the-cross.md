I know that there are some faces that I’m seeing in the sanctuary today who were here with us yesterday. And if you were here with us yesterday, then I am certain beyond a doubt that you had something delicious to eat. And if you were not here yesterday, then following the liturgy, I know that you will be gathering en masse to storm the hall and take part in all of the goodies that have been prepared over these past many months for the food festival.

Now, I have to say that I am blessed to be a firsthand witness to all of the hard work and industriousness that makes a food festival happen. I have witnessed that over the past few days, over the past few months, and over these past many, many years that I have been blessed to be the parish priest of St. John Armenian Church. Somebody was cooking the pilaf this morning before the sun was rising. Somebody was chopping onions a few days ago that are going to be in the yalanchis that you’re eating. Somebody was baking choreg many weeks ago that you are going to love with your coffee this afternoon. And well before that, there were people like Sona Tomasian and Emilia Sarabian and Sona Tanzer, and so many, many, many more. God have mercy on me for even starting to mention names, because I can’t say everyone’s. But the fact of the matter is that we have seen all of the hard work that is necessary for our eighty-first food festival to come together.

But this morning, we’re going to ask ourselves a broader question, and that question is: What does it take for a church to come together?

Today is a special day in our church. It is the Feast of the Discovery of the Holy Cross, Gyut Khachi, and our readings point us back to the cross today time and time and time again. St. Paul tells the Corinthians that we preach Christ crucified, and this Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God.

This morning, though we didn’t read it during the liturgy, we also had as part of our lectionary the Book of Wisdom. In that, we read about a man who trusts himself to a piece of wood on the open sea. The Church hears this and thinks, of course, of the cross—not because the author was predicting the Holy Cross, but because it is the same God who saves through wood on the water who is saving us through wood on the hill of Golgotha.

Talking about the cross is talking about the God who loves us, who cared for us and provided for us, and still does. He gave that piece of wood, the Holy Cross, which is like a ship that carries us through the rough waters of this life in the form of a church—this church, this parish—which carries us in the spirit of the Holy Cross. He didn’t wait for us to become worthy. He didn’t ask us to prove ourselves. Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ gave himself on the cross for you and for me, and that is where the Church begins. It doesn’t begin with our work and our effort. It begins with his gift and his grace, and everything that we do for this parish is a response to that gift, not a payment for it.

In today’s Gospel reading, Christ is speaking with his disciples about his second coming. He says that the sign of the Son of Man will appear in heaven, and the Church Fathers have long seen that as the sign of the Holy Cross itself, shining over all of creation. So, on this day, we celebrate finding and discovering the Holy Cross. The Gospel tells us that the cross is also what we are going to discover at the very end of this life.

Then Christ says that he will send out his angels, and they will gather his people from the four winds, from one end of heaven to the other. As I look out on this congregation today, I see my brothers and sisters in Christ who know something about being scattered, and who are still being scattered to this day. As we look upon this congregation, we see that almost all of us have come from somewhere else. And if we have not, then we have parents and grandparents who have. Across different generations, different stories, different accents, and different ports of call, we have come together. We didn’t make ourselves into one family in this parish, because it is Christ who has called us to him and to one another. That is his work, not ours. And once he has gathered us, he teaches us to belong to each other.

So here is the question for you and me today: Christ has gathered us here together in the shadow of his Holy Cross as we rediscover it. He has given us a ship to cling to, a cross to guide us, and a hope to carry us. If Christ is gathering us to himself and to one another, how are we supposed to live together while we wait for him?

In today’s Gospel reading, Christ answers that very same question just a few verses later. He asks, “Who then is the faithful and wise servant whom his master has set over his household to give them their food at the proper time?” Like maybe after Badarak today.

Notice how very ordinary and familiar that sentiment is. It is not some great miracle or some fantastic work. It is food given at the right time for the people of the house. That is what faithfulness looks like when we wait upon the Lord: you take care of the people and what has been entrusted to you.

When St. Luke tells this same teaching, he calls the faithful servant a steward. In our Armenian Bible, we call this person a Dndes, the one entrusted with the care of the household. He doesn’t own it. It belongs to Another, with a capital A, yet it is entrusted to him for a time.

I want to stop here for a second, because this is very important for us today. This is a word that you are going to be hearing very often at St. John going forward. For a long time in the Armenian Church, we have talked about being a member of a parish. This is not the belonging that Christ has described for us.

Today, we are changing how we speak about belonging here. We won’t be asking you to become a dues-paying member or a pledged member of the church—a concept that undoubtedly, for many of you sitting in the pews today, still sounds foreign and strange. We are going to invite you to become a Dndes, a steward of God’s house, just as God has called us to be. That house is our parish. That house is St. John.

Every one of us who has been baptized and chrismated in the name of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit has already been made part of God’s family, a member of his body. That is a gift. It is something that we cannot earn and cannot buy. Dndes is an invitation to put that belonging into practice by caring for our parish and for one another.

That care takes four forms. We worship together. We pray together. A good steward has to attend to the master, hear his voice, and respond to it. For us, that is a worshipful life.

We serve together by giving of our time and our gifts. This looks like a thousand different things. But service is effort; it is work. Christ has not called us to be passive in his house. We are called to participate and to be part of the life of a parish.

And we are called to pledge—to give. We give because this is how we show how much it means to us. As the Gospel tells us, the kingdom of God is like finding a precious pearl in a field, and the one who finds it then sells everything he owns so that he may go and buy it. We are meant to give through our life in the Church, not out of necessity, but because it is the source of blessings in our lives. But we are only called to give as much as is according to our means.

Many of you are able to do this, and you are already doing it. Many of the people already doing this may not yet be classified as dues-paying members of St. John Armenian Church. You do it quietly and lovingly, and many do not notice. I know that you don’t do it for recognition. You do it because it is fulfilling and because it is God-pleasing. All I can say is: keep doing what you’re doing.

Some of you are carrying a lot on your shoulders right now. I know that—with your health, your family life, your work, your responsibilities, and the cares of this world. I want you to hear me clearly: the Lord does not measure your love by the size of what you give. You offer what you can, when you can, with a grateful heart.

Over the next few Sundays, I will continue talking about this idea that we call being a Dndes in the Armenian Church—being entrusted with the care of our spiritual home. For this week, I want to leave you with one question: How do you care for St. John Armenian Church, and how might you care for it more faithfully?

Let us pray with that question, and let us look for one small answer—something real that each and every one of us can do. And remember where all of our care begins. It begins at the foot of the cross, with the One who cared for us, who is carrying us, and who gathers us to himself, for the glory of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. Amen.
